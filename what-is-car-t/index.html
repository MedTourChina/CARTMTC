What Is CAR-T Therapy?

Your own immune cells, re-engineered into a targeted cancer treatment — and infused back as a single dose.

A "Living Drug" Made From Your Own Cells

CAR-T stands for Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell therapy. It is a type of immunotherapy that takes a patient's own T cells — a kind of white blood cell that fights disease — and reprograms them in the laboratory to recognize a specific marker (antigen) on cancer cells.

🧬

T cell → add a synthetic receptor (CAR) → multiply → infuse → seek & destroy cancer

Why It Changed Cancer Care

Before CAR-T, many patients with relapsed or refractory blood cancers had exhausted every standard option. CAR-T has produced complete remissions in people for whom chemotherapy, targeted drugs and stem-cell transplants had failed.

Because the engineered cells can persist in the body for months or years, they may continue to guard against the cancer long after a single infusion — which is why CAR-T is often described as a "living drug."

CAR-T vs. Conventional Treatment

Understanding how CAR-T differs helps patients and families set realistic expectations.

AspectChemotherapy / RadiationCAR-T Cell Therapy
What it targetsAll rapidly dividing cells (including healthy ones)A specific cancer marker (e.g. CD19, BCMA)
SourceManufactured drug given repeatedlyThe patient's own cells, engineered individually
CourseMultiple cycles over weeks/monthsTypically a single infusion
DurabilityEffect stops when treatment endsEngineered cells may persist and protect for years
Key risksSuppressed immunity, hair loss, nauseaCRS, ICANS (mostly manageable in expert centers)
Educational note: CAR-T is not suitable for every patient or every cancer. Eligibility depends on the specific diagnosis, prior treatments, organ function and overall fitness. A specialist review of your records is the first step.

Key Terms, Simply Explained

🧬

T cell

A white blood cell of the immune system. CAR-T starts with these cells collected from the patient's blood.

🎲

CAR (receptor)

A synthetic protein added to the T cell so it can "see" and bind a specific antigen on cancer cells.

🏷

Antigen (target)

The marker the CAR recognizes — most commonly CD19 (B-cell cancers) or BCMA (myeloma).

🌡

CRS

Cytokine Release Syndrome — a fever/inflammatory response after infusion, usually manageable with dedicated care.

🧐

ICANS

Immune Effector Cell-Associated Neurotoxicity — a temporary neurological effect, monitored closely by the team.

🨂

Remission

Disappearance of detectable disease — the primary goal, achieved in many relapsed/refractory cases.

Autologous vs. Allogeneic ("Off-the-Shelf")

Most approved CAR-T today uses the patient's own cells (autologous). The next frontier is allogeneic CAR-T from donor cells, which could be ready without a weeks-long manufacturing wait.

🧬
Available now

Autologous CAR-T

Made from the patient's own T cells. Highly personalized; requires a manufacturing period of a few weeks between collection and infusion.

🩹
Clinical research

Allogeneic CAR-T

Built from healthy donor cells and engineered to be "universal." Aim: off-the-shelf availability. Mostly still in trials, with some early approvals emerging.
