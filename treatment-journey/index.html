Managing the Two Side Effects That Matter

Almost every CAR-T conversation eventually reaches the same question: how sick will I get? These are the two complications the treating team watches for, how they are graded, and what the largest safety dataset actually shows.

🌡
CRS

Cytokine release syndrome

The engineered cells multiply fast and release inflammatory cytokines. The result is fever, and in more severe cases low blood pressure and low oxygen. It usually begins within days of infusion and is reversible with treatment.

🧠
ICANS

Neurological symptoms

ICANS covers confusion, word-finding difficulty, handwriting changes, headache and, rarely, seizures. It is far less common than CRS and, in the autoimmune cohort described below, occurred in 3% of patients and was always reversible.

How CRS is graded, and what happens at each grade

The grade is not a label for how the patient feels. It is a decision rule that determines which drug is given and whether care moves to intensive care.

CRS grading and standard management
Grade What it looks like What is usually done
1 Fever only — no low blood pressure, no oxygen requirement Observation, fluids, cooling; often nothing else
2 Fever plus either low blood pressure that responds to fluids, or mild oxygen need Tocilizumab, sometimes a short course of steroids; monitoring continues
3 Low blood pressure needing vasopressors, or oxygen needing a higher-flow device Intensive care, tocilizumab plus steroids, close organ monitoring
4 Life-threatening: shock, or oxygen need that mechanical support cannot solve Critical care; rare in autoimmune cohorts
Read the distribution, not the headline.
In the largest autoimmune CAR-T safety cohort published to date — 108 patients — 80% developed CRS. That number is quoted everywhere. What usually gets left out: 66% of the entire cohort had grade 1 only, and exactly one patient reached grade 3. The untreated figure sounds alarming; the graded figure is a managed side effect.
The fever is not the therapy working.
Patients who developed CRS and patients who did not had remission rates of 78% and 91% respectively — a difference that was not statistically significant. There is no evidence that a harder reaction means a better response, and no reason to want one.
Two blood tests, and one question to ask about them.
Baseline B-cell count and baseline IL-6 both independently predicted who would develop CRS. Patients above both cutoffs developed it 100% of the time; patients below both, 43%. But the tests in that study were drawn at the eligibility visit, while patients were still on immunosuppressive drugs — which lowers the B-cell count for reasons that have nothing to do with the disease being quiet. If a clinic quotes you a risk tier, ask when the blood was drawn and what you were taking that week. A risk tier without a timestamp is not a risk tier.
Why autoimmune dosing differs from cancer dosing.
Autoimmune protocols generally use lower cell doses and milder lymphodepletion than lymphoma protocols, because the goal is to deplete a B-cell compartment rather than to eliminate a tumour bulk. That is one reason the toxicity profile in the table above looks gentler than the lymphoma experience — and one reason results cannot simply be transferred between the two.
