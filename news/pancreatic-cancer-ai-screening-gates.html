An AI That Spots Pancreatic Cancer a Year Early, and the Gate That Must Come First

November 8, 2026Beyond CAR-T7 min read
The algorithm sees what radiologists cannot. The harder problem is deciding who should be scanned at all.

A 63-year-old man had a contrast-enhanced abdominal CT that was reported as normal. Two years and five months later he was diagnosed with advanced pancreatic cancer. When Mayo Clinic researchers re-read the old scan with an AI model called REDMOD, the region the software flagged sat exactly where the tumour later grew. The case, published in Gut in 2026, is the most persuasive illustration of a claim now circulating widely: that artificial intelligence can find pancreatic cancer more than a year before a clinician can.

That claim is broadly true and still incomplete. The model was trained and tested on scans of people who had already been selected as high risk. Its performance numbers describe what happens inside a narrow funnel, not what happens if a hospital points it at everyone who walks in. This article sets out the funnel, the numbers, and the two specific reasons a Chinese version of this programme cannot simply be imported. We cover it here not because it is a CAR-T therapy, because it is not, but because the same reading skill applies to every cell therapy headline: ask which patients the evidence actually describes.

What REDMOD actually detected

The team collected 219 CT scans that looked normal to the human eye but came from people diagnosed with pancreatic cancer three to 36 months later. In an independent test set, the model identified 46 of 63 such patients, roughly seven in ten, while two senior abdominal radiologists working together found about four in ten. In a larger 493-person test set, sensitivity was 73.0 percent against 38.9 percent for the radiologists, with specificity of 81.1 percent. For scans taken more than 24 months before diagnosis, the model still found 68.4 percent, against 23 percent for the readers. The mean lead time was 475 days.

The model does not look for a mass. It measures the microscopic texture of the whole pancreas, extracting 968 radiomic descriptors of grey-level and granular pattern and reducing them to 40 filtered features that the eye is not sensitive to. It also picks up a small loss of pancreatic volume: median 68.9 millilitres in eventual cases versus 74.7 millilitres in controls. One useful analogy is a piece of cloth that has not torn but whose weave has begun to loosen; only a systematic measurement would notice.

Two limits sit next to those results. Specificity of 81.1 percent means close to one in five healthy scans is flagged, and the model has mainly been validated inside Mayo Clinic with no published external multi-institutional validation. REDMOD is a research tool, not an FDA-cleared diagnostic, and it needs portal-venous-phase contrast CT. None of that is a small footnote when the proposed use is screening.

The short version

The tool: REDMOD, an AI radiomics model that reads pancreatic texture on contrast-enhanced CT scans taken before a visible tumour appears.

The performance: 73.0 percent sensitivity and 81.1 percent specificity in a 493-person independent test set, with a mean lead time of 475 days.

The prerequisite: patients must first be selected by new-onset diabetes plus an ENDPAC score of 3 or higher.

The status: research use only, with a 100-person feasibility trial due to complete initial validation in 2029.

Why a blood-sugar gate comes first

Pancreatic cancer is usually found late. The pancreas sits behind the stomach and against the spine, and early lesions produce almost no pain. Mayo Clinic cites figures of more than 85 percent of patients already having metastatic or locally advanced disease at diagnosis and five-year survival below 15 percent. Chinese 2022 data recorded about 118,700 new cases and 106,300 deaths, a near one-to-one ratio that reflects how rarely the disease is caught early.

Blood glucose is one of the few signals that appears beforehand. A subset of patients develops a sharp rise in glucose one to two years before diagnosis, often labelled ordinary type 2 diabetes. The proposed biology is that the tumour disrupts the pancreas and its glucose regulation, so the diabetes is a consequence rather than a cause. The clinical clue is counterintuitive: instead of the weight gain common in type 2 diabetes, these patients lose weight as their glucose climbs.

The epidemiological anchor is a 2005 Mayo study from Minnesota reporting a 0.85 percent three-year incidence of pancreatic cancer in people over 50 with new-onset diabetes, about eight times the rate in peers. A 2026 prospective cohort of nearly 19,000 people refined this further: a glycaemically defined date of diabetes onset preceded diagnosis by about eight months on average. The sequence is therefore glucose first, then imaging, then AI, with the AI reading scans taken on average 475 days before diagnosis.

ENDPAC: the second, narrower gate

Age over 50 plus new-onset diabetes is still far too broad. In that group fewer than one person in a hundred develops pancreatic cancer within three years, so a screening programme built on that criterion alone would generate an enormous number of false alarms. Chari and colleagues built ENDPAC to tighten the funnel. It scores three things: how much weight has been lost, how fast glucose rose within a year, and the age at which diabetes was diagnosed.

In validation, a score of 3 or above raised the three-year probability to 3.6 percent, 4.4 times that of an unselected new-onset diabetic, and captured seven of nine eventual cases. About 49 percent of patients scoring zero or below were classified as very low risk. The model performs less well in non-white populations, which matters directly for the question of applying it in China.

The arithmetic that decides whether screening helps

Screening is a numbers game about prevalence, not just model accuracy. Imagine 10,000 people who have passed both gates. At 3.6 percent prevalence, about 360 of them have the disease; with 73 percent sensitivity the AI finds roughly 263, while its 81 percent specificity produces about 1,822 false alarms among the healthy. That is one true case for every eight alerts, tolerable for a high-risk programme with a clear follow-up path.

Now remove the gates and scan 10,000 unselected adults. Pancreatic cancer is rare enough that there may be fewer than one true case in the group, yet the model still produces roughly 1,890 false alarms. Every one of those alarms can lead to endoscopic ultrasound, an invasive procedure in which a probe is passed through the stomach to sit beside the pancreas, along with anxiety and cost. The gate decides how many real cases exist; the AI decides how many healthy people get frightened.

Mayo's prospective AI-PACED study (NCT07324096) shows where the real bottleneck is. It enrolls people aged 50 to 85 with diabetes diagnosed within 180 days and an ENDPAC score of at least 3, running the AI silently in the background. In the September 2026 progress report, an automated electronic-record search surfaced 253 candidates; after confirmation there were 175 people with genuine new-onset diabetes, and only 16 of those reached the score threshold. The hardest part of the programme is finding the right people, not reading the pictures.

Two parts that must be rebuilt for China

Chinese adult diabetes prevalence is now about 12.4 percent, so the potential pool is large. But the risk model does not transfer unchanged. A large Chinese prospective study found that pancreatic cancer risk rises with longer diabetes duration, unlike the Western pattern in which the first years after diagnosis are the most dangerous. Only 36.7 percent of people with diabetes in China are aware of their diagnosis, which makes the idea of new-onset hard to define from records alone. And in a US prospective cohort, three-year risk after age 50 was 0.84 percent in white participants but 0.22 percent in Asian participants, less than a third as high. A Chinese ENDPAC would need recalibration against a local cohort, not a translated cutoff.

The imaging component is the second mismatch. REDMOD was built on contrast-enhanced CT, while China's largest-scale early-detection efforts, including the PANDA model, work from non-contrast plain CT, which is far more widely available and easier to deploy in population screening. Whether the same microscopic texture signal survives without contrast has no published evidence yet. A realistic Chinese route would use the continuous glucose records already held by health-check centres to define onset, recalibrate the score locally, and then test for texture signal in the large archives of existing plain scans.

For patients reading this from outside China, one practical conclusion follows. None of these tools is a bookable clinical service. REDMOD is not approved for diagnostic use, ENDPAC is a research score, and the Mayo feasibility study is planned to finish initial validation in 2029. A clinic offering AI pancreatic cancer screening as a paid add-on to a medical-travel package should be asked which model, which validated population, and which regulator approved it.

Where this leaves CAR-T

This is a diagnostics story, not a cell therapy story, and we label it that way deliberately. The only CAR-T product approved in China for a solid tumour is licensed for advanced gastric or gastro-oesophageal junction adenocarcinoma after at least two lines of therapy, which is late-stage disease, not screen-detected disease. Earlier detection would eventually change the stage mix of patients who might be considered for cell therapy, but it changes no approved indication today.

The transferable lesson is about reading evidence. A 73 percent sensitivity figure sounds like a verdict on a technology; it is actually a conditional statement about a carefully selected group. The same habit applies to response rates, survival curves and first-patient-treated announcements in cell therapy. Always ask what population sat in front of the number.

  1. Has the model been validated outside the institution that developed it, and on the scanner protocol used at the centre you are considering?
  2. Does the programme define high risk with a documented new-onset diabetes date and a validated risk score, or does it simply offer scans to everyone?
  3. Is the CT protocol contrast-enhanced, and if a plain scan is used, is there published evidence that the texture signal still holds?
  4. What is the written follow-up path for a positive AI flag, and who pays for endoscopic ultrasound and repeat imaging?
  5. Is the service an approved diagnostic, a research study with ethics approval, or a commercial screening add-on?
  6. Does the clinic separate the price of the scan from the rest of a treatment package?

This article is educational and is not medical advice. A new finding of raised blood glucose after age 50 is far more likely to be ordinary type 2 diabetes than pancreatic cancer; unexplained weight loss, upper abdominal or back pain, or jaundice alongside it should be discussed promptly with an endocrinologist or gastroenterologist.
